**There are more US $100 bills in circulation than $1 bills.** At the end of 2025, the Federal Reserve counted **19.9 billion $100 notes** in circulation and **15.2 billion $1 notes**.

## The numbers

The Fed's table counts every note in circulation, in billions, as of December 31 each year. For 2025:

- **$100: 19.9 billion notes**, the most of any denomination.
- **$1: 15.2 billion.**
- **$20: 11.0 billion.**
- **All notes: 56.6 billion.**

That means about 35% of all US notes in circulation are $100s (our math: 19.9 divided by 56.6).

## How it flipped

Twenty years ago, the $1 was far ahead:

- **2005:** 8.8 billion $1 notes, 5.4 billion $100s.
- **2016:** 11.7 billion $1s, 11.5 billion $100s. Still a $1 lead, barely.
- **2017:** 12.5 billion $100s, 12.1 billion $1s. The $100 pulls ahead.
- **2025:** 19.9 billion $100s, 15.2 billion $1s.

From 2005 to 2025, the number of $100 notes grew about 3.7 times, while $1 notes grew about 1.7 times (our math from the Fed table).

## Why $100s pile up

The Fed's answer is in how people use them. On its page about note lifespans, it says "larger denominations such as $100 notes are often used as a store of value, which means they pass between users less frequently than lower-denominations such as $5 notes, which are more often used for transactions."

That shows up in how long notes last. The Fed estimates, as of May 2025:

- **$100 note: 24.0 years** on average.
- **$1 note: 7.2 years.**
- **$5 note: 5.8 years**, the shortest of any denomination.

## The limits

- **The count is notes in circulation, not notes in daily use.** The Fed's table does not say where the notes are held or how often they change hands.
- The Fed says its lifespan figures are estimates of average life, and that it updated its method in 2025. Actual life varies with how a note is used.
- This is US currency only.
