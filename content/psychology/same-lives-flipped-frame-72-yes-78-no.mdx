**Would you rather save 200 lives for sure, or take a one-third chance to save 600? Most people pick the sure save. Reframe the same math as deaths, and most people flip to the gamble.**

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman called this the Asian disease problem in a 1981 Science paper. An unusual disease is expected to kill 600 people. Two programs are proposed. The arithmetic is identical. Only the wording changes.

---

## Lives saved

**Problem 1 (N = 152):**

- Program A: **200 people will be saved.** Chosen by **72%**.
- Program B: **1/3 chance that 600 are saved, 2/3 chance that nobody is saved.** Chosen by **28%**.

## Deaths

**Problem 2 (N = 155):**

- Program C: **400 people will die.** Chosen by **22%**.
- Program D: **1/3 chance that nobody dies, 2/3 chance that 600 die.** Chosen by **78%**.

Saving 200 is the same as 400 dying when 600 are at risk. A one-third chance everyone is saved is the same as a one-third chance nobody dies. The majority still reversed.

## Why it matters

The paper's point is not that people are careless. Frames change which outcomes feel like gains or losses. Sure gains look attractive. Sure losses look repulsive. That is a would-you-rather with stakes, and the frame picks the winner.
