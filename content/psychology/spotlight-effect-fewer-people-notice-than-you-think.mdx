**Spilled coffee on your shirt? Bad hair day? Far fewer people noticed than you think.**

Psychologists call it the **spotlight effect**: we tend to believe others notice our looks and our actions much more than they do. The name comes from a paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 2000 by Thomas Gilovich of Cornell University, with Victoria Husted Medvec of Northwestern University and Kenneth Savitsky of Williams College.

## The embarrassing T-shirt test

In the first study, Cornell students were asked to put on a T-shirt with a large picture of singer Barry Manilow, "a musician who is not terribly popular among college students," the authors wrote. Each wearer walked into a room where other students were filling out a questionnaire, then was sent back out after a moment.

Asked how many people in the room could say who was on the shirt:

- The wearers guessed **46%** on average.
- The real share was **23%**.

The wearers' average guess "was exactly twice as high as the average accuracy rate of the observers," the paper says.

## Even a shirt you like

In a second study, people picked a shirt they were happy to wear, with a famous face of their choice. They guessed **48%** of the room would notice. Only **8%** did. That guess was "six times as great as the observers' actual accuracy."

## Getting used to it helps

In a later study, people wore the Barry Manilow shirt either right away or after sitting in it for 15 minutes. Those who walked in right away guessed **51%** of observers would notice. Those who had time to get used to it guessed **37%**. The authors say we start from our own strong sense of the shirt and do not adjust enough for how little others are paying attention.

The same pattern showed up in group discussions: people thought their own slip-ups and good points stood out more to the group than they did.

## What the study can and can't tell you

- **The samples were small.** Each of the two T-shirt studies had 15 shirt wearers, and the participants were US college students.
- **It is from 2000.** The basic finding is old and well known, but these exact numbers come from those small studies.
- **It is a tendency, not a rule.** Some people do notice. Just fewer than you fear.

As the authors put it, people "seem to believe that the social spotlight shines more brightly on them than it truly does."
