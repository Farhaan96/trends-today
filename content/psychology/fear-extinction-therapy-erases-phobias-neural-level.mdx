Why do some fears fade with time and practice while others keep coming back? Neuroscience research in mice points to part of the answer. **MIT researchers** found that dopamine acts as an "all-clear" signal that helps the brain learn a danger has passed, a process called fear extinction.

**Fear extinction does not delete a fear memory. In mice, a new extinction memory forms in reward-linked neurons of the posterior basolateral amygdala and competes with the original fear memory. A 2025 MIT study found that dopamine from the ventral tegmental area drives that learning. Exposure therapy, which relies on extinction, is a well-supported treatment for specific phobias.**

This helps explain why fear can return after it seems to be gone. Fear memories and extinction memories are held by separate groups of neurons that compete for control of behavior. ## Two Neural Populations Compete for Control

The amygdala holds opposing memories that influence whether an animal freezes in fear or moves on. **MIT neuroscientist Susumu Tonegawa** and his team identified the specific neurons involved, in a 2020 study in **Neuron**.

In mice, fear memories are encoded by neurons in the anterior basolateral amygdala that express the **Rspo2 gene**. When the mouse later learns that a place is no longer dangerous, neurons in the posterior basolateral amygdala that express **Ppp1r1b** encode a new extinction memory. Those same neurons also respond to reward.

The researchers concluded that fear extinction memory is a newly formed reward memory. MIT says this may help explain why it feels good to realize an expected danger has passed. The work was done in mice, so how closely it maps onto human therapy is still an open question.

## The Reconsolidation Window

Timing may also matter. When a memory is recalled, it can become unstable for a short period before being stored again, a process called reconsolidation. A 2010 study in **Nature** by Daniela Schiller, Elizabeth Phelps, Joseph LeDoux and colleagues at New York University tested this in **65 healthy volunteers** who had learned to fear a colored square paired with a mild shock.

Participants who were reminded of the fear memory **10 minutes** before extinction training showed no return of fear the next day. Those reminded **6 hours** before extinction, or not reminded at all, showed fear coming back.

Key findings from the study:

- **10 minute group**: No spontaneous return of fear a day later
- **6 hour group and no-reminder group**: Fear returned
- **Follow-up**: In a smaller group tested **10 to 14 months** later, the effect was still present

Animal research suggests this window lasts about six hours. The study measured a laboratory-conditioned fear in healthy adults, not a clinical phobia. A 2016 meta-analysis of later studies found that this "post-retrieval extinction" approach had a small to moderate added effect in humans compared with standard extinction.

## Dopamine Acts as the Safety Signal

In the 2025 study, published in **PNAS**, a team led by former Tonegawa lab members **Xiangyu Zhang** and **Katelyn Flick** traced where the extinction signal comes from in mice.

They found that dopamine neurons in the ventral tegmental area send more connections to the Ppp1r1b extinction neurons than to the Rspo2 fear neurons. Dopamine activity at the extinction neurons rose when mice stopped freezing, and mice with the strongest signal learned extinction best. Using optogenetics, the team showed that silencing this dopamine input impaired fear extinction and boosting it sped extinction up. Activating dopamine input to the fear neurons instead stopped the fear from fading.

The authors note that fear extinction involves many brain areas, not just this one circuit. They suggest the circuit could be a useful target for research into anxiety and PTSD. ## What This Means for Treatment

Exposure therapy already relies on extinction learning, and it has strong research support. The Society of Clinical Psychology (APA Division 12) rates exposure therapies for specific phobias as having **strong research support**.

What the research shows:

- **Large effects**: A 2008 meta-analysis of 33 randomized trials found exposure-based treatment produced large effects compared with no treatment, and outperformed placebo and other psychotherapies
- **In vivo exposure**: Facing the real feared object or situation outperformed imaginal or virtual reality exposure at the end of treatment, though not at follow-up
- **Durability**: Treatment gains are generally maintained for about a year, but longer follow-up studies are limited
- **Drawbacks**: In vivo exposure is linked to high dropout rates and low treatment acceptance

Pairing a brief memory reminder with exposure sessions is being studied as a way to make results last longer, but it is not yet standard practice. The MIT team points to the posterior basolateral amygdala and dopamine signaling as possible targets for future treatments. Exposure therapy works by using the same learning systems that create fears in the first place. Fear is not deleted from the brain. Instead, a new safety memory competes with the old one, and research in mice suggests dopamine helps that new memory form.
