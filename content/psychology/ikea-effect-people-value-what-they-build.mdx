**People value things more when they build them themselves.** In a study published in the Journal of Consumer Psychology, people who assembled a plain IKEA storage box bid **$0.78** for it. People handed the identical box, already built, bid **$0.48**. That is about **63% more** for the same box (our math).

The researchers, Michael Norton of Harvard Business School, Daniel Mochon and Dan Ariely, named it **the IKEA effect**.

## The box experiment

In the first experiment, 52 participants at a US university were split at random. "Builders" got a flat-packed black IKEA Kassett storage box and its instructions. "Non-builders" got the same box, fully assembled, and could inspect it.

Then everyone bid on the box, using a method that makes people state what they would really pay: bid at or above a randomly drawn price and you buy the box at that price.

- **Builders bid $0.78** on average.
- **Non-builders bid $0.48.**
- Builders also liked the box more: 3.81 out of 7, against 2.50.

## The origami twist

The second experiment asked a bigger question: how much do people overvalue their own work? Some of 106 participants folded an origami frog or crane, then bid on keeping it.

- **Builders bid $0.23** on their own origami.
- **Strangers bid $0.05** on those same creations. The paper says they saw them as "nearly worthless crumpled paper."
- **Another group bid $0.27** on origami folded by experienced folders.

So novices valued their own creations almost as highly as others valued an expert's. The paper found no significant difference between those two bids.

## You have to finish

In a later experiment, 39 participants worked on the same IKEA box. Some built it all the way. Others were stopped before the last two steps.

- **People who finished bid $1.46.**
- **People stopped short bid $0.59.**

The boost held even for people who said they were not "do-it-yourself" types. In a separate experiment with Lego sets, people who built a set and then took it apart no longer valued it significantly more.

## It shows up in kids too

Later research led by L. E. Marsh and colleagues found the bias emerging around age 5. A 2022 study of 128 children aged 5 and 6 in the UK and India found that children in both countries valued a toy they had assembled over an identical copy.

## The limits

- **The samples were small and drawn from universities:** 52, 106 and 39 people in the experiments above.
- The dollar amounts are small, because the items were cheap. The finding is about the gap between bids, not about what furniture is worth.
- Figures here come from the Harvard Business School working paper version of the study.
