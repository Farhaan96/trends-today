**The same face looks a little more attractive in a group photo than when you see it alone.** Drew Walker and Edward Vul at UC San Diego called this the cheerleader effect and found it in five experiments.

It is not that group members are actually better looking. Any given face in the squad scored higher when rated from the group photo than from a cropped solo portrait of that same face.

---

## How big is the bump?

Across hundreds of faces, the bump was small but consistent:

- **Female faces:** about **5.5% of a standard deviation** more attractive in a group than alone (Experiment 1).
- **Male faces:** about **5.6% of a standard deviation** (Experiment 2).

Subjects rated 300 unique faces twice each: once in a three-person same-gender group photo, and once as an isolated portrait.

## Why it happens

The paper's account has three steps:

1. Your visual system automatically builds an **ensemble average** of the faces in the group.
2. Each individual face is **biased toward that average**.
3. **Average faces look more attractive** than most of the faces that go into them.

So a face in a group is pulled a bit toward a prettier average, and you rate it higher.
