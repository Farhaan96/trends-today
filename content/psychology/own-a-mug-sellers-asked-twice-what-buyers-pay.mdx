**Randomly give half a class a coffee mug, then open a market. Owners ask for about twice what buyers will pay. That ownership bump is the endowment effect.**

Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler ran the classic Cornell version. Bookstore price on the mug was $6.00. In four mug markets, the median owner would not sell for less than $5.25, while the median buyer would not pay more than $2.25 to $2.75.

---

## How thin the trading got

If ownership did not change value, about half the mugs should trade. With 22 mugs, that prediction was 11 trades. Observed trades: 4, then 1, then 2, then 2. Induced-value token markets run the same way traded near the predicted volume, so the gap was not from confusion about the rules.

## Why it feels that way

The papers treat the bump as loss aversion. Giving up a mug you own is coded as a loss. Getting the same mug is coded as a gain. Losses loom larger, so owners demand more dollars than buyers will offer.

A later Simon Fraser design compared sellers, buyers and "choosers" who picked between a mug and cash without owning one first. Sellers still sat near $7; choosers and buyers clustered near $3. Ownership, not the cash constraint, drove the gap.
