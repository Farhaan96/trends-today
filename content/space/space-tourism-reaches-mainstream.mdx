Private individuals have flown to space, but calling space tourism "mainstream" overstates the market. Flights remain infrequent, prices are out of reach for most travellers, and announced schedules can change.

The useful way to understand the industry is to separate **suborbital flights happening now**, **services companies plan to restart or introduce**, and **orbital missions arranged for private participants**.

## Suborbital and orbital trips are different

A suborbital flight rises high enough for passengers to experience several minutes of weightlessness and see Earth against the darkness of space, then returns without circling the planet.

An orbital mission must reach much greater speed and stay in space long enough to travel around Earth. It requires more training, time, and operational support. These are not interchangeable products.

## What Blue Origin currently offers

Blue Origin uses its New Shepard vehicle for suborbital missions. The company says its [NS-38 mission](https://www.blueorigin.com/missions/ns-38) flew six people on January 22, 2026.

Prospective customers can submit a flight application through Blue Origin's [New Shepard experience page](https://www.blueorigin.com/new-shepard/experience). The company asks for a **US$150,000 refundable deposit** to begin the order process. Its own terms state that the deposit does not guarantee a seat.

That distinction matters. A deposit is not a published final ticket price, a confirmed departure date, or proof that thousands of people are booked to fly.

## What Virgin Galactic says is next

Virgin Galactic is developing its next generation of Delta-class spacecraft. In its [first-quarter 2026 business update](https://investors.virgingalactic.com/news/news-details/2026/Virgin-Galactic-Announces-First-Quarter-2026-Financial-Results-and-Provides-Business-Update/default.aspx), the company said it was working toward flight testing in the third quarter of 2026 and a first spaceflight in the fourth quarter.

Those are company targets, not completed milestones. Anyone considering a future booking should check the latest flight-test results, commercial terms, refund conditions, and regulatory status before paying.

## How U.S. passenger safety oversight works

Commercial human spaceflight is not regulated like airline travel. The [U.S. Federal Aviation Administration](https://www.faa.gov/space/human_spaceflight) licenses launch and re-entry operations primarily to protect the public on the ground and in the air.

The FAA says spaceflight participants must give informed consent after being told about the risks. It also states that the U.S. government does not certify a commercial launch vehicle as safe for carrying people.

That means a provider's licence is not a government safety endorsement. Prospective passengers should read risk disclosures carefully and ask what training, medical screening, insurance, emergency procedures, and refund rights apply.

## Questions to ask before paying

Before treating an application or deposit like a normal travel booking, verify:

- Is the trip suborbital or orbital?
- Is the quoted amount a deposit, reservation fee, or total price?
- Is the flight date confirmed or only a company target?
- What happens if testing or operations are delayed?
- Is the deposit refundable, and under what conditions?
- What training and medical screening are required?
- What insurance or liability waivers apply?

## The accurate bottom line

Space tourism is real, but it is still an early commercial activity rather than mass travel. A small number of private participants have flown, one provider is accepting applications, and another is preparing a new vehicle.

Readers should be skeptical of precise waiting-list totals, bargain ticket claims, orbital hotel opening dates, and celebrity quotes unless they can be traced to current primary sources. In this market, a plan is not a flight and a deposit is not a ticket.
